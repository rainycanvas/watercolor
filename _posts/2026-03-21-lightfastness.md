Lightfastness measures how long the color stays vibrant before fading. This is normally measured under "museum conditions".

Museum conditions normally mean indirect light, UV filtering, and a stable climate. The home condition could be much worse. If you hang your painting in a sunroom or near a window, the fading could happen much faster. Also as far as I know, most museums won't have the same artwork on exhibition throughout the entire year. They rotate pieces into dark storage to protect them.

<h2>Lightfastness Standard</h2>

When you check a paint tube for its lightfastness rating, you will likely encounter one of two major international standards: ASTM or the Blue Wool Scale.

ASTM standard uses a Xenon Arc lamp to simulate intense sunlight in a lab. Blue Wool Scale is a comparative test where the paint is tested alongside eight strips of blue-dyed wool.

| Blue Wool  | ASTM | Lightfastness |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 7 - 8 | I | over 100 years |
| 6 | II | 50 - 100 years |
| 4 - 5 | III | 15 - 50 years |
| 2 - 3 | IV | 2 - 15 years |
| 1 | V | less than 2 years |

If you plan to sell your work or display it in a gallery, I highly recommend sticking to pigments with ASTM I or II (Blue Wool 6-8) ratings. Your clients and collectors expect their investment to last for decades without significant fading. If you are simply practicing, working in a sketchbook (where the pages are closed and protected from light), or painting specifically to scan and digitize your work, then extreme lightfastness is not a strict requirement.

<h2>Do Your Own Test</h2>

Many professional brands use the standard ASTM or Blue Wool ratings. However, some manufacturers use their own internal systems, like a simple 1 to 3-star scale, without clearly explaining how they map to international standards.

If lightfastness is critical to your work, the best way to be certain is to do your own test. Simply create your own swatch, cut the swatch in half or cover one half with a think, light-proof material, then place the exposed half under direct sunlight. After a few months, compare the exposed side and the protected side.

It is also highly beneficial to test lightfastness in both masstone (concentrated color) and tints (diluted with water). In the commercial world, lightfastness ratings are almost always based on the masstone. However, some pigments that appear "Excellent" when applied thickly can fade significantly faster when diluted into a pale wash.

If you discover that your own fade test results contradict the official rating on the paint tube, don't worry - it is not uncommon LOL. Some brands are simply not so rigorous with their labeling. Furthermore, even if different brands use the exact same pigment code (e.g., PV23), their quality can vary significantly depending on the pigment supplier. The resulting lightfastness can also vary between brands.

<h2>What Happens When Paint Fades</h2>

Fading doesn't always mean a color simply becomes a lighter version of itself. Depending on the pigment's chemical structure, "fading" can look very different.

- Losing Saturation: Some vibrant colors don't disappear entirely but become dull and "muddy," shifting toward a desaturated, earth-like tone.

- Color Shifting: A complex mixture might change its hue. For example, a convenience green made of a stable blue and a fugitive yellow will eventually turn into a pale blue as the yellow component disappears.

- Complete Disappearance: Some organic or "fugitive" pigments (like certain traditional carmines or neon pinks) can vanish completely, leaving nothing but the white of the paper behind. 

Unlike most pigments that fade permanently, Prussian Blue has a strange ability to fade and then recover. Many brands label it as an excellent lightfast paint, which could be misleading.

<h2>Other Causes of Fading</h2>

Some pigments are highly sensitive to their environment. For example, Ultramarine Blue (PB29) can "bleach" or lose its color if exposed to acidic conditions. Conversely, other pigments might shift or darken if the environment is too alkaline. This is why using acid-free, 100% cotton paper is non-negotiable for professional work.

Some pigments will 'help' others fade. Zinc White (PW4), often labeled as Chinese White, is known to accelerate the fading of other organic pigments when mixed with them. If you mix a semi-stable red with PW4 to make a pastel pink, that pink might fade much faster than the red would have on its own.

Some pigments fade without even exposed to light. The famous Opera Pink contains fugitive dyes, which is chemically unstable. Even if you keep your sketchbook closed in a dark drawer, the fluorescent brightness can diminish over time due to heat, humidity, or simply the natural breakdown of the dye molecules.

<h2>After the Fade</h2>

Ancient pigments were mostly earth and minerals. If a red paint fades, scientists can scan the surface for Iron (Fe) or Mercury (Hg). If they find Copper (Cu), they know the faded patch was once a vibrant blue or green (like Malachite or Azurite). These heavy metals act like "fingerprints" that never disappear, allowing us to digitally restore the original colors of history.

Modern organic pigments (like many of our bright Quinacridones or Phthalos) present a unique challenge for the future. Unlike mineral paints, many synthetic organic colors don't just dull down - they can vanish completely. Because these pigments are organic, they are primarily composed of Carbon (C), Hydrogen (H), and Oxygen (O), the same basic building blocks as the paper they sit on. They normally have excellent lightfastness, but once those paints fades away, a future archaeologist might find absolutely nothing to analyze. Therefore, there is a growing movement toward High-Fidelity Digital Scanning.
