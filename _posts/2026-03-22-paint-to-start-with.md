The variety of watercolor paints is staggering, with some brands offering over 200 colors. However, you don't need a massive collection to start. While it’s possible to paint with just 3 primary colors, I believe 8 to 12 colors is the "sweet spot" - it provides a perfect balance between mixing convenience and avoiding an overwhelming palette.

<h2>Primary Triads</h2>

The minimum starting point are the primaries. 

|  Name  | Pigment Code |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Yellow** | PY97 or PY3 |
| **Magenta** | PV19 or PR122 |
| **Cyan** | PB15:3 or PB16 |

Here is the visualization of the three primary colors on the color wheel. (taken from artistpigment.org)

<img src="/watercolor/assets/img/primary_triads.jpg">

Everything seems perfect so far, but only in theory. In the physical world, there is no such thing as a perfect primary pigment. Every mix comes with a "Saturation Cost". The further apart two colors are on the color wheel, the more "vibrancy" they lose when forced together. If you mix a Magenta with a Yellow to get an orange color, it will not look as glowing or punchy as a single-pigment orange like Pyrrole Orange (PO73).

Beyond the physics of it, mixing exclusively with a primary triad is notoriously complicated. Because every single hue on your paper must be engineered from different ratios of just three tubes, it demands an immense amount of technical knowledge and experience. For a beginner, this is a massive hurdle.

Some artists claim that since every hue is derived from the same few colors, the painting will automatically be harmonious. To be honest, I don't see the logic in that. In reality, you can easily mix plenty of clashing, discordant colors from just three primaries. Harmony usually comes from a limited gamut - think of it like applying a specific filter to a photograph to unify the tones. The goal of a primary triad, however, is exactly the opposite: it aims to expand the gamut as much as possible.
